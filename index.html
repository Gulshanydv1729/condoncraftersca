Section 02 • Biological Source & Function

What is Insulin?

Understanding the hormone that regulates blood glucose levels, how it unlocks cellular energy, and why external insulin replacement is essential in diabetes.

Fundamental Definition

What is Insulin?

Insulin is a vital peptide hormone produced naturally by the Beta (β) cells located within the Islets of Langerhans in the human pancreas. Its primary role is to regulate blood glucose levels so body energy remains balanced.

Molecular Composition: Two polypeptide chains (A-chain with 21 amino acids and B-chain with 30 amino acids) linked by disulfide bridges.
Physiological Mechanism

What Does It Do in the Body?

After a meal, carbohydrates are digested into glucose, which enters the bloodstream. Insulin acts as a molecular key that binds to insulin receptors on cell membranes, allowing glucose to enter muscle, liver, and fat cells to produce cellular energy (ATP).

Homeostasis: Maintains normal blood glucose levels around 5.6 mmol/L and stores excess glucose as glycogen in the liver.
Clinical Necessity

Why is it Needed in Diabetes?

In Diabetes Mellitus, the body either fails to produce sufficient insulin due to beta-cell destruction (Type 1 Diabetes) or cannot effectively utilize the insulin produced (Type 2 Diabetes). Without external insulin replacement, glucose builds up dangerously in the blood.

Life-Sustaining Therapy: External recombinant human insulin restores normal glucose uptake and prevents severe organ complications.

Pancreas Islets of Langerhans Beta Cells (B)

The human pancreas contains specialized endocrine tissue clusters known as the Islets of Langerhans. Within each islet lies a cluster of Beta cells (B) that naturally produce insulin and carry the human insulin gene sequence.

02. ISLET CLUSTER

Important Clarification (Slide 01): The pancreas is shown as the biological source of the human gene. Commercial recombinant insulin is produced using engineered microorganisms — not by repeatedly removing cells from a human pancreas.

How We Make It — 6-Step Production Process

A comprehensive step-by-step visual guide illustrating how recombinant human insulin is engineered, fermented, purified, tested, and packaged.

Step 01 • Slide 02: Genetic Information & cDNA Synthesis

Step 1: Gene Selection & cDNA Synthesis

The original human insulin gene DNA sequence holds the instructions for producing insulin protein in human beta cells. To express this gene efficiently in microorganisms, scientists use processed mature mRNA (with non-coding introns removed) to synthesize complementary DNA (cDNA) containing only the functional protein-coding exons.

1. Human Insulin Gene

Original DNA sequence carrying genetic instructions for making human insulin.

2. Mature mRNA

Processed transcript carrying the protein-coding sequence with introns removed.

3. Why cDNA?

cDNA contains only coding exons, simplifying expression by excluding non-coding introns.

Diagram 01: Genomic DNA → Mature mRNA → Coding cDNA
Step 02 • Slide 02: Vector Insertion & Recombinant DNA

Step 2: Inserting cDNA into a Plasmid Vector (Recombinant DNA)

A circular bacterial DNA molecule called a Plasmid Vector acts as a genetic carrier. Using restriction enzymes to open the plasmid ring and DNA ligase to seal the ends, the human insulin cDNA coding sequence is spliced into the plasmid vector, creating a complete Recombinant DNA (rDNA) molecule ready for host transfer.

Vector / Plasmid DNA Carrier

Circular plasmid acts as a carrier vehicle to transport the human cDNA sequence into the host cell.

Recombinant DNA Construct

Combined plasmid vector + human insulin cDNA insert, engineered for microbial expression.

Diagram 02: Plasmid Vector + cDNA Insert → rDNA
Step 03 • Slide 03: Host Cell Biological Production System

Step 3: Host Cell Transformation (E. coli or Yeast)

The recombinant plasmid is introduced into a safe microbial host cell such as E. coli bacteria or yeast. Once inside, the engineered host cell acts as "a small biological factory", using the inserted genetic instructions for gene expression to synthesize insulin-related protein chains.

1. Host Cell

Non-pathogenic laboratory bacterium (E. coli) or yeast serves as the host organism.

2. Recombinant DNA

Inserted plasmid genetic material replicates inside the multiplying host cells.

3. Gene Expression

Host ribosomes translate the genetic code into human insulin protein chains.

Step 04 • Slide 04: Controlled Bioreactor Fermentation

Step 4: Bioreactor Fermentation (Exponential Cell Growth)

Engineered production cells are cultured inside a sterile stainless-steel Bioreactor. By maintaining strictly controlled environmental conditions, the cells multiply exponentially to produce large quantities of target protein with consistent quality.

1. Temperature Stable Maintained at 37°C

Optimal thermal condition for cell growth.

2. pH Controlled Balanced at pH 7.0

Neutral pH maintained for cellular health.

3. Nutrients — Sugar Glucose Feed

Supplies continuous energy for dividing cells.

4. Oxygen Availability Sterile Aeration

Ensures sufficient dissolved oxygen supply.

5. Uniform Mixing Continuous Impeller Stirring

Ensures even nutrient and temperature distribution for exponential cell proliferation.

Step 05 • Slides 05 & 06: Harvesting, Recovery & 4-Stage Purification

Step 5: Harvesting & 4-Stage Downstream Purification

Following fermentation, production cells are collected (1. Harvest), opened conceptually to release intracellular protein (2. Disruption), and separated into a crude extract (3. Recovery). This mixture then undergoes a rigorous 4-Stage Purification process to isolate pure human insulin.

01

Clarification

Removes larger unwanted cellular debris from the harvested mixture.

02

Separation

Chromatography separates insulin-related protein from other host components.

03

Concentration

Concentrates the desired active human insulin protein fraction.

04

Final Polishing

Additional ultra-purification so the product meets pharmaceutical testing standards.

Section 06 • Slide 09: Smart Packaging & Patient Support

Storage, Smart Packaging & Safety Information

Connecting the product with the person who uses it through proper cold-chain storage guidelines and our proposed SMART PACK (TEMP-QR-INFO) concept.

Essential Cold-Chain Guidelines

Recommended Storage, Temperature & Expiry

1. Unopened Vials & Pens: 2°C to 8°C (Refrigerated)

Store unopened insulin vials and pens in a refrigerator between 2°C and 8°C until their printed expiration date. Do not freeze—freezing permanently denatures the insulin protein.

2. In-Use Vials & Pens: Below 25°C–30°C (Up to 28 Days)

Once opened, in-use insulin can be kept at controlled room temperature (below 25°C–30°C) for up to 28 days away from direct heat and sunlight.

3. Expiry Date & Visual Inspection

Always verify the expiration date printed on the label and inspect the solution before use to ensure regular human insulin remains clear, colorless, and free of particles.
